## Process is the part people underestimate

Everybody looking for a branding partner is looking at portfolios. Fair — you should like the work.

But the work you see in a portfolio is the output. What determines whether *your* project ends up looking like that is the process, and specifically whether there is one. I have seen good designers produce mediocre brands because the project had no structure: unclear decision-maker, feedback arriving in three separate emails from three people, strategy skipped in the interest of getting to logos faster.

So instead of showing you more work, here is exactly how we run a branding project at BLURRD. Week by week, what happens, what we need from you, and where things go wrong when the process is loose.

## Before we start: the fit conversation

Every project starts with a 15-minute call. Not a sales pitch — a fit check.

What I am trying to learn: what is actually driving this. "We need a rebrand" is never the real reason. The real reason is usually one of these:

- We are raising and the brand does not match the story we are telling investors
- We have outgrown the identity we made ourselves in year one
- We are moving upmarket and our brand prices us too low
- We merged, or added a product line, and now we have two brands fighting
- Our category shifted and we look like the old version of it

Those lead to different work. A company moving upmarket needs a different project than a company unifying two identities. Getting the driver right in the first conversation is what keeps the whole project pointed the right way.

I am also checking for one thing that predicts success more than budget or timeline: **is there a single decision-maker?** Projects with a clear owner go well. Projects where five stakeholders each get an equal vote produce compromise design, every time.

## Week one: strategy

This is the week people want to skip. It is the week that makes everything after it possible.

We are not producing a 40-page brand strategy deck nobody reads. We are answering a handful of questions clearly enough that design decisions become defensible instead of subjective.

**Positioning.** Who you are for, what you do, and why it matters to them. Specific enough to exclude someone. If your positioning could apply to your three closest competitors, it is not positioning yet.

**Audience.** Not a demographic profile. What the person is actually trying to do, what they are worried about, and what would make them trust you in the first five seconds.

**Competitive landscape.** What everyone in your category looks like. This one is genuinely useful in Vegas, because a lot of local categories have a very strong visual convention — hospitality, real estate, trades — and you have to decide deliberately whether to fit in or stand apart. Both are valid strategies. Doing it by accident is not.

**Personality and voice.** Three to five attributes with real edges. "Professional" is not one. "Direct, unfussy, warm" gives a designer something to work with.

What we need from you this week: an honest hour or two of conversation, access to whoever knows your customers best, and any research or feedback you already have sitting around. If you have sales call recordings, those are gold.

By the end of week one there is a short document we both agree to. Every design decision after this gets measured against it. That document is what turns "I don't love the blue" into a productive conversation.

## Weeks two and three: identity

Now design starts.

We present **two or three developed directions**, not a wall of options. Each one is a real point of view — a logo, a type system, a color palette, and enough application to see how it behaves in the world. A mark on a white slide tells you nothing. A mark on a sign, an app icon, a business card, and a website header tells you everything.

The presentation is a conversation, not a reveal. I walk through the reasoning: here is the strategy, here is how this direction expresses it, here is where it is strong and here is its tradeoff. Every direction has a tradeoff. Pretending otherwise is how clients get talked into something that fails in application later.

Then we narrow to one direction and refine. Refinement is real work, not polish: testing the mark small, checking it in one color, making sure the type holds up at body sizes, verifying color contrast passes accessibility requirements on real backgrounds.

How we handle feedback in this phase matters as much as the design:

- Feedback comes back **consolidated**, from one person, in one place
- We ask for reactions framed against the strategy, not personal preference
- Two rounds of refinement, which is enough when the strategy is solid
- Decisions get written down so week five does not reopen week three

The thing I ask clients to resist is the mid-project stakeholder. Somebody who was not in the strategy conversation seeing logos for the first time in week three will have opinions untethered from any of the reasoning. Include them early or trust the person who was there.

## Weeks four and five: the system

A logo is not a brand. This phase is where the identity becomes something your team can actually use.

**Type system.** A scale with defined sizes, weights, and line heights. What headlines use, what body uses, what small print uses. Web-licensed fonts sorted out, because discovering a licensing problem after launch is a bad day.

**Color system.** Roles, not just swatches — primary, surface, border, muted text, accent. Contrast checked. This is the same discipline I described in [design systems for growing Vegas companies](/blog/design-systems-for-growing-vegas-companies), and doing it during branding means the website build inherits it instead of reinventing it.

**Application.** Your real touchpoints, not generic mockups. If you are a hospitality brand that means menus, signage, and staff apparel. If you are a SaaS company it means app icon, product UI, and slide templates. If you are a service business it means vehicle graphics, invoices, and proposal documents. We build the ones you actually use.

**Guidelines.** Short and practical. Clear space, minimum sizes, what not to do, and correct examples. Twenty useful pages beats an eighty-page manual nobody opens.

## Rebrand or refresh

Worth saying out loud, because a lot of companies book a full rebrand when they need something smaller.

A **refresh** keeps your equity and tightens the execution. Same name, recognizably the same mark, but a cleaned-up logo, a real type system, a better palette, and guidelines that did not exist before. Two to three weeks. This is the right call when customers already know you and the problem is inconsistency rather than identity.

A **rebrand** changes the strategic position and the visual expression together. New mark, possibly new name, and a rollout plan because you are asking the market to re-learn you. Four to six weeks minimum, plus the migration work after.

How I tell them apart in the fit call: if the answer to "what do people get wrong about you" is a factual detail, that is a refresh. If the answer is "they think we are a different kind of company than we are," that is a rebrand. The second one cannot be solved by better typography.

There is no prestige in the bigger project. Doing the smaller one well is frequently the better spend.

## Where branding projects go sideways

Honest list, from experience.

**Skipping strategy to get to logos faster.** Feels efficient in week one. In week four you have no basis to choose between directions except taste, and taste arguments do not resolve.

**Design by committee.** Every stakeholder getting equal veto produces something nobody hates and nobody is proud of.

**Chasing the current look.** There is always a trend. Adopting it wholesale means a brand that is legibly dated in three years. Some borrowed contemporary language is fine; building the whole identity on it is not.

**Stopping at the logo.** You get a mark, no system, and six months later every piece of collateral is a different interpretation of it.

**No handoff to development.** The brand is beautiful in Figma and approximated in code. Whoever builds the site needs tokens and rules, not a PDF. That is the connection point between branding and the actual build, and it is where a lot of otherwise good brand work gets lost.

## What being in Las Vegas actually changes

Two real things, not a marketing angle.

First, we can be in the room. Kickoff and the identity presentation are better in person — you read reactions you would never catch on a call, and decisions happen faster. If you are here, we meet.

Second, we know the market. We have seen what the hospitality, gaming, real estate, and service categories here look like, which means we can tell you when a direction is going to blend into the wall of your competitors. That is genuinely useful context, and it is hard to get from a studio that has never worked in this city.

## Takeaways

- Nail the real driver behind the rebrand in the first conversation.
- One decision-maker. This predicts project success more than anything else.
- Strategy week is what makes design decisions defensible instead of subjective.
- Two or three developed directions beats ten thumbnails.
- Feedback consolidated, from one person, in one place.
- The system and the handoff are what make a brand survive contact with your team.
