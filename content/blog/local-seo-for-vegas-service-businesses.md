## When someone's AC dies in July, nobody scrolls

It hit 114 in Henderson last summer and I watched a neighbor stand in his driveway calling HVAC companies off his phone. First one didn't pick up. Second had a full voicemail box. Third answered and had a tech out that afternoon at a premium rate he didn't argue about.

That's local SEO in Las Vegas. Not a keyword report. A guy in a driveway with a hot house and about ninety seconds of patience.

I've built and rebuilt sites for service businesses across the valley, and the pattern holds: the companies winning locally aren't the ones with the most content. They show up in the map pack, load fast on cell data, and make the next step obvious. Here's what I'd actually do, in order.

## The three things Google is actually weighing

Strip away the noise and local ranking comes down to three inputs, and they don't carry equal weight.

**Proximity** — how close you are to the person searching. This is the one nobody wants to hear because you can't optimize your way out of geography.

**Relevance** — how clearly your profile and site say "we do this exact service."

**Prominence** — reviews, mentions, links, and general signals that you're a real established business.

You control relevance and prominence completely. Proximity you control once, when you sign a lease. That's worth knowing before you spend a dime.

## Your Google Business Profile does more work than your homepage

I'll say the uncomfortable part first: for a service business in Las Vegas, your Google Business Profile drives more calls than your website. People tap the phone number straight from the map result. Your beautiful hero section never loads.

So treat the profile like a product, not a checkbox.

**Primary category is the single biggest lever.** Not "Contractor." Pick the specific one — "HVAC contractor," "Medical spa," "Personal injury attorney." Then add secondary categories for the adjacent stuff. I've seen a category change alone move a business from position eight into the top three, because it was miscategorized for two years.

**Fill the services list out completely.** Every service, with the description field actually written, not left blank. This is free relevance and most competitors skip it.

**Photos, on a schedule.** Not a stock dump on day one. Real job photos added a few times a month — trucks in Summerlin driveways, before and after on a repipe, your team. Profiles with regular photo activity get more calls and direction requests, and it just looks like a business that's still operating.

**Answer the Q&A section yourself.** You can post your own questions and answer them. "Do you charge for diagnostics?" "Do you service Boulder City?" That's exactly what people want to know, and it shows up right in the result.

**Use posts for real things.** Seasonal offers, new services, hiring. Weekly is fine. Skip it and you've left a slot empty that your competitor is filling.

## Proximity is the constraint you plan around, not fight

Here's what trips up every service-area business in the valley: you serve all of Clark County, so you assume you can rank across all of it. You can't. Your profile has one pin, and results shift as the searcher moves.

Someone searching in Summerlin gets a different map pack than someone in Anthem. If your shop is off Sunset and Eastern, you're strong in the southeast and thin in the northwest. That's it. That's the mechanic.

What actually helps:

- **Pick your address deliberately.** If you're signing a lease, run searches from a few candidate zip codes first. Central Spring Valley or the Sunset corridor puts more of the valley within reach than the far northwest.
- **Build location depth on your own site**, where you're not limited to one pin.
- **Ask for reviews that name the neighborhood.** "Came out to Southern Highlands same day" is a relevance signal you didn't have to write yourself.
- **Second location only if it's real.** Staffed, with signage and its own phone. Virtual mailboxes get suspended, and the suspension takes your reviews with it.

## One page per thing you want to be hired for

The most common structural mistake I see: a single `/services` page with twelve bullet points. That page ranks for nothing because it's about nothing in particular.

Each service you'd happily take a call about gets its own page. Real page, not a stub. On it:

- What the service actually is, in the words your customers use, not industry terms
- What it costs, or at minimum a range and what moves it
- How long it takes and what the process looks like step by step
- Photos from jobs you've actually done
- Three to five FAQs answering what people ask on the phone
- One clear next step

That last one matters. If the page ends without a phone number or a booking link in view, you built a brochure.

The reason this doesn't turn into an unmanageable pile of work is components. Build the service page template once, with a real system behind it, and page eleven takes an hour instead of a week. ## Neighborhood pages, done in a way that works

Location pages have a bad reputation because most of them deserve it. Twenty pages, identical copy, city name swapped. Google's been filtering those out for years, and now they'll drag the good pages down with them.

The version that works has no shortcut. For each area you genuinely serve — Summerlin, Henderson, North Las Vegas, Spring Valley, Enterprise, Anthem — the page needs things that could only be true about that area: jobs you've done there with photos, details specific to the housing stock (older Henderson homes have different plumbing problems than 2019 builds in Inspirada, so say that), a review from someone local, and your real response time from the shop.

If you can only write four of those honestly, write four. Four real pages beat twenty thin ones.

## Reviews are the thing you can fix this week

Of everything on this list, review velocity is the fastest lever you have, and it's the one most businesses handle by accident.

Some specifics that matter:

**Recency beats total.** A profile with 60 reviews where the last one came in yesterday outperforms 300 reviews that stopped in 2024. Google reads the dead profile as a business that might not exist anymore.

**Ask at the right moment.** Not in a follow-up email three days later. The tech asks when the customer is visibly happy — AC blowing cold, invoice signed — and texts the link right there. That's the difference between a 10% and a 40% hit rate on asks.

**Respond to every single one.** Positive ones in a sentence. Negative ones carefully and without arguing. People read the responses more than the reviews, and a calm reply to a one-star does more for you than the one-star costs.

**Never buy them.** Suspensions in this market are real, and reinstatement takes months you don't have.

## Speed matters more here than the average market

Most Vegas service searches happen on a phone, outside, on cell data, often from a car. That's the worst possible conditions for a slow site, and it's your primary traffic.

The number I watch is Largest Contentful Paint — how long until the visitor sees something real. Under 2.5 seconds on a mid-range Android on LTE, not on your MacBook on office fiber. Test it that way or you're testing a fantasy.

Where sites usually lose it: uncompressed hero images, a pile of third-party scripts, chat widgets loading before content, fonts blocking render. None of it is hard to fix, it's just usually nobody's job.

## Track it, or you're guessing

You need to know which calls came from where. Minimum setup: a tracking number on the site, the real number on your Google profile, Search Console for queries by page, and profile insights for calls. One monthly note on whether calls for your top three services went up is enough.

## What I'd skip

- **Citation blast packages.** Do the ten that matter — Google, Bing, Apple Maps, Yelp, BBB, your industry associations — and stop.
- **Daily blog posts about nothing.** Ten thin posts do less than one great service page.
- **Keyword-stuffed footers.** "HVAC repair Las Vegas Henderson Summerlin Paradise" in 8px gray text hasn't worked in a decade.
- **Rank tracking as your main metric.** Rankings shift by neighborhood and device. Calls are the metric.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does local SEO take to work in Las Vegas?

Profile fixes and review momentum show up in three to six weeks. Ranking a new service page organically is more like three to six months, longer in the brutal categories where everyone's spending.

### Do I need a physical address to rank in Las Vegas?

You need a real one to have a profile, but service-area businesses can hide it. What you can't work around is proximity, so if you're moving offices, treat it as a marketing decision too.

### Are neighborhood pages for Summerlin and Henderson worth building?

Only with unique proof on each — real jobs, photos, local specifics. Templated pages with the city swapped are worse than not having them.

### How many Google reviews do I need to compete?

Match the top three in your map pack, then keep going, because recency counts as much as total.

### Does site speed affect local rankings?

Indirectly. It affects conversion directly, which is the part that pays you. Six seconds on LTE loses the call before rankings matter.

## Bottom line

Local SEO for a Vegas service business isn't complicated, it's just unglamorous. Fix the profile properly. Get reviews every week. Build one strong page per service. Make it fast on a phone. Know which calls came from where.

Do those five things consistently for six months and you'll be ahead of most of your competitors, because most of them will do two of the five and quit.
