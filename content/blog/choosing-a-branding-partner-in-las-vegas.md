## The buying experience is genuinely confusing

I'll say the obvious thing up front: I run a studio that does this work, so I'm one of the options you'd be evaluating. Read this knowing that, and hold me to the same list.

Because the market is a mess. You can pay $150 on a marketplace or $150,000 to a national agency for something described with the same word. There's a solo designer in the Arts District, a marketing agency off Sahara that also does brand, a Webflow shop that'll throw in a logo, and a guy on Instagram with great mockups and no process. All of them say "branding."

I've been on both sides of this — hired designers, been the hired designer, and cleaned up after projects that went badly. So here's the actual evaluation framework, including the parts that are inconvenient for me to say.

## The four kinds of people who'll pitch you

**Marketplace and template.** A few hundred dollars, days not weeks. You get a logo file. No strategy, often no source files, and no guarantee it's not sold to someone else. Genuinely fine if you need a mark for a side project and nothing more. Not fine if you're building something you intend to grow.

**Solo freelancer.** One person, direct access, usually strong craft in one area. Best value in the market when the fit is right. Risks: capacity when they get busy, a gap in whatever they're not great at, and no continuity if life happens.

**Small studio.** A few people covering strategy, design, and build. More expensive than a freelancer, considerably cheaper than an agency, and generally the sweet spot for businesses between one and fifty people. This is what I run, so discount my enthusiasm accordingly.

**Full agency.** Account managers, research, media, multiple designers. Real value if you need enterprise coordination or paid media alongside brand. Also more layers between you and whoever's actually designing, and you pay for those layers.

None of these is the right answer universally. What's wrong is paying agency rates for freelancer output, or expecting agency scope from a marketplace.

## What you're actually paying for is decisions

Here's the reframe that helps most people: you're not buying files. You're buying decisions you don't have to revisit.

A good branding engagement leaves you knowing who you're for, what you say, what you look like, and — critically — how to apply it next time without calling anybody. The files are just where those decisions get stored.

That's why "how many logo concepts do I get" is the wrong question. Twenty concepts means twenty unmade decisions handed back to you. Two or three developed directions with real reasoning means somebody did the thinking.

## Questions to ask before you sign

I'd ask all of these. Anybody good will have answers ready.

- **"Walk me through a project like mine from kickoff to handoff."** You're listening for a process, with named phases. Hesitation here is the biggest tell there is.
- **"What do I actually receive?"** Get the file list. Logo formats, source files, color specs, type files, templates, guidelines doc.
- **"How many revision rounds, and what counts as one?"** Unlimited revisions is not generous, it's undefined. Two or three structured rounds is healthy.
- **"Who's doing the work?"** At an agency, the person pitching is often not the person designing. Fine, but know it.
- **"What do you need from me, and when?"** A partner who can't answer this will blame you for delays later.
- **"Show me something that didn't go well and what you changed."** The best answer I've ever gotten to this question came from someone who told me plainly about a project they lost and why. Hired them.
- **"Do you build the site too, or hand off?"** If handoff, ask what the developer receives. Bad handoff is where most brand work loses its quality.

## Red flags

- **A price without a conversation.** If someone quotes your brand before understanding your business, they're selling a package.
- **Portfolio that's all unbuilt concepts.** Beautiful mockups of brands that never shipped. Ask which ones launched and how they're doing.
- **No contract, or a one-pager.** Scope, timeline, payments, ownership. All four in writing.
- **Vague about file ownership.** Anything that sounds like a licensing arrangement on your own logo, walk away.
- **Won't say no.** A partner who agrees with every suggestion isn't a partner. You're paying for judgment, and judgment sometimes disagrees with you.
- **Talks entirely in feelings.** "Elevated, curated, disruptive." Ask how the work will affect something measurable. If there's no answer, there's no thinking.
- **Wildly cheap for the scope described.** Somebody's cutting something. Usually it's the strategy, occasionally it's originality.

## Green flags

- Asks about your business model and margins, not just your favorite colors
- Pushes back on your brief early with better questions
- Shows work in context — signs, screens, packaging, actual applications
- Has a real guidelines document from a past project they can show you
- Talks about what they'd skip to fit your budget, honestly
- Gives you a timeline with dates on it
- Has one or two clients you can actually call

That last one is underused. Two ten-minute calls with past clients will tell you more than any portfolio review.

## What the price ranges actually mean

Rough and honest, for this market:

**Under $2,000** — a logo. Possibly a good one. No strategy, minimal system, limited applications. Appropriate for a side business or something you're testing.

**$3,000 to $8,000** — a real identity for a small business. Positioning conversation, logo system, color and type, a handful of applications, a short guidelines doc. This is where most Vegas small businesses should land.

**$10,000 to $30,000** — a full system for a funded startup, multi-location business, or anything with several audiences. Research, naming if needed, deeper applications, thorough guidelines, usually website design alongside.

**Above that** — agency scope. Multiple stakeholders, formal research, sub-brands, campaign work.

The main thing: know which tier your business actually needs. Overspending on brand before you have a working offer is a common and expensive mistake. Underspending when you're about to raise or scale is also expensive, just later.

## Get the ownership question in writing

Boring and important. Before you pay anything, confirm:

- You own the final logo and identity assets outright, on final payment
- You receive editable source files, not just exports
- Font licensing is spelled out — fonts are usually licensed to you separately and don't transfer automatically
- Photography and illustration usage rights are defined
- If they use your work in their portfolio, that's normal, but you can ask about timing

I've watched a business get held up because the designer who made their logo went dark and had the only editable file. Twenty minutes of paperwork prevents that entirely.

## Does local actually matter?

Sometimes, genuinely.

Local helps when the work depends on local knowledge — hospitality, real estate, home services, anything competing neighborhood by neighborhood. Someone who knows the difference between how Summerlin and North Las Vegas read a brand is bringing something a remote designer can't Google. I got into that specifically in [brand identity for Vegas real estate teams](/blog/brand-identity-for-vegas-real-estate).

Local also means you can meet, see printed work in person, and get referrals from people you actually know.

But if you're building something national or entirely online, don't limit yourself to a 30-mile radius. Process and craft beat proximity. Just make sure you've got a real communication rhythm — a standing weekly call, one place decisions get recorded — because remote projects fail on communication far more often than on skill.

## Run the project well and you'll get more for your money

Half of a project's outcome is on the client. The best engagements I've had shared the same traits:

- **One decision maker.** Committee feedback averages a brand into mush. Gather input, but one person decides.
- **Feedback on the goal, not the taste.** "This won't read on a yard sign at 40 mph" is useful. "I don't love blue" is not.
- **Fast responses.** A week of silence on a review costs you a week of timeline. Every time.
- **Real inputs.** Actual customer language, real photos, honest sales data. Great work needs real material.
- **Trust the process, once.** If you hired for judgment, let the judgment run at least one full round before overriding it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much should branding cost in Las Vegas?

Mid four figures for a solid small business identity, low to mid five figures for a full system. Under two thousand you're buying a logo.

### Should I hire local or go remote?

Local when local context matters — hospitality, real estate, service businesses. Remote is fine for national or online brands if the communication rhythm is real.

### Do I own the logo and source files when it ends?

You should, in writing, on final payment. Ask specifically about editable files and font licensing.

### How many logo options should I expect?

Two or three developed directions with reasoning. Twenty options means the decision got handed back to you.

## Bottom line

Ask about process, not portfolio. Get the deliverable list and the ownership terms in writing. Match the price tier to what your business actually needs right now. Call a past client. And pick someone willing to tell you no.

The best branding partner for you is the one who understands your business well enough to disagree with you about it.
