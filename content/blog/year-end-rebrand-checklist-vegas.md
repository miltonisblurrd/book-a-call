## The December window nobody uses

Every year I get the same email around February. Some version of: "We want to rebrand — can we have it done before the show in April?"

And every year I have to explain that no, six weeks is not enough to do strategy, identity, and a site rebuild well, and rushing it produces exactly the kind of forgettable work you were trying to escape.

The teams that get this right start in December. Not because December is magical, but because of how Vegas actually works.

![Year-end rebrand checklist for Las Vegas businesses](/images/blog/year-end-rebrand-checklist-vegas-og.png)

Q1 here is the densest stretch of the year. CES in January pulls a huge share of the tech and hospitality world into town. World of Concrete, the gaming and nightclub shows, the hospitality circuit — depending on your category, a meaningful chunk of your annual pipeline gets created in a ten-week window. If your brand is going to change, you want it changed and settled before that, not mid-flight.

Meanwhile December is when new budgets get approved, when leadership is around and reachable before the holidays, and when your team has slightly more slack than they will have again until summer.

So here is the checklist. Part one is deciding whether you should. Part two is what to do if the answer is yes.

## Part 1: Should you actually rebrand?

Be honest through this section. A rebrand you do not need is an expensive way to feel productive.

**Has what you sell changed?** Not "we added a service." Genuinely changed — you started as a web shop and now you are a product company, you sold to consumers and now you sell to enterprises. If the business changed and the brand did not, you have a real problem.

**Has who you sell to changed?** Moving from small local clients to regional or enterprise accounts is the most common trigger I see here. A brand that read as approachable and scrappy can quietly cost you deals when the buyer is a procurement team.

**Are you losing to companies you are better than?** If you consistently lose to competitors whose actual work is weaker, that is often a perception problem, and perception is what branding addresses.

**Does your team avoid sharing the brand?** Watch what your salespeople do. If they build their own decks instead of using the company template, that is a verdict. Same if nobody puts the logo on their LinkedIn banner.

**Is the name a liability?** Hard to spell, hard to say, too geographically narrow, colliding with a bigger company in search results. Name problems compound and only get more expensive to fix.

**Are you just bored?** Genuinely — this is the one to sit with. You look at your brand daily. Your customers see it a handful of times a year. Boredom is not a business case. Your recognition is worth more than your fatigue.

If you counted two or more real yeses, keep reading. One yes is probably a refresh. Zero is a strong signal to spend the budget on content and photography instead.

## Part 2: What to do, in order

### Weeks 1 to 2: Gather evidence

**Interview eight to twelve people.** Customers, prospects who chose someone else, and your own sales team. The lost prospects are the most valuable and the most uncomfortable. Ask what they thought you did before they talked to you. The gap between that and reality is your brief.

**Audit your competitors visually.** Put twelve local and regional competitors on one board. You will usually find a dominant sameness — everyone using the same blue, the same stock photography, the same tone. That sameness is your opening.

**Inventory every place the brand exists.** Site, social profiles, email signatures, proposals, invoices, decks, signage, vehicle wraps, uniforms, trade show booth, packaging, the sign in your lobby. Write it all down now. This list drives your launch plan and it is always three times longer than anyone guesses.

**Pull your numbers.** Traffic, conversion rate, average deal size, win rate. Baseline them so you can measure whether this worked. Rebrands get judged on feelings otherwise, and feelings are not a defense at a board meeting.

### Weeks 3 to 4: Lock positioning

Before anything visual. Who you serve, what you do, why it is different, and how you sound. It should fit on one page. If it takes ten pages, it is not decided yet.

The hardest part is narrowing. Everyone wants to serve everyone. The brands that cut through pick a lane and accept losing the business outside it. That is the whole trade.

If you are earlier stage and this is your first real pass at positioning, [branding for Las Vegas startups](/blog/branding-for-las-vegas-startups) covers the foundational version of this work.

### Weeks 5 to 8: Identity

Logo, type system, color system, photography direction, graphic language, voice guidelines. Two or three directions, not ten. Ten directions means the strategy was not clear enough to constrain the design.

Two things I push for on every project. Test the logo small before you approve it — most brands are seen at 24 pixels in a browser tab more than anywhere else. And build the type and color system as roles rather than fixed values, so it can carry into your product or app later. That is exactly the alignment problem I wrote about in [brand and product alignment for Vegas tech](/blog/brand-and-product-alignment-for-vegas-tech).

For the full breakdown of deliverables and what each phase produces, [what a Las Vegas rebrand includes](/blog/las-vegas-rebrand-whats-included) has the detailed scope.

### Weeks 6 to 12: Applications, running in parallel

Start applications before the identity is fully signed off. Waiting for perfect approval before touching the website is how February launches become May launches.

Priority order, based on what your buyers actually see:

1. Website — highest visibility, longest lead time
2. Sales collateral — decks, proposals, one-pagers
3. Social profiles and email signatures
4. Physical — signage, vehicles, print, booth graphics

If the website is being rebuilt rather than restyled, run my [website redesign checklist](/blog/las-vegas-website-redesign-checklist) alongside this one. The two projects share a critical path and coordinating them saves weeks.

### Launch week: Everything at once

This is the part teams get wrong most often, and it is entirely avoidable.

Pick a single date. Update everything digital that day — site, social, email signatures, Google Business Profile, directory listings, review platforms. Send the announcement to customers before they discover it on their own. Brief your team beforehand so nobody is answering questions they have not been prepped for.

Physical items can lag. Digital cannot. A partial digital launch means weeks of looking like two companies, which is worse than not having launched at all.

**Do not change your domain unless you must.** If you do, you need every URL mapped with 301 redirects, updated citations everywhere, and patience for a temporary ranking dip. Plan for it rather than discovering it.

### Weeks after: Hold the line

The first ninety days determine whether it sticks. Somebody has to own consistency — reviewing decks, catching the old logo on a form nobody remembered, keeping the voice steady. Without an owner, brands drift back within a year.

Check your baseline numbers at ninety days. Track qualitative signals too. Are inbound leads better fits? Do prospects arrive with fewer misconceptions? That is often where you feel it first.

## Budgeting it without guessing

The number that surprises people is not the identity work. It is the applications.

Strategy and identity are a defined scope — interviews, positioning, logo, type, color, guidelines. Reasonably predictable. What blows up budgets is the tail: the website, the sales deck, the proposal template, the trade show booth graphics, the vehicle wraps, the lobby signage, the branded folders somebody ordered five thousand of last year.

That is why the inventory in week one matters so much. Once you have the full list, sort every item into three buckets. Must change before launch — anything a customer sees in the buying process. Change within ninety days — internal documents, secondary collateral. Change on natural replacement — printed inventory, uniforms, anything with existing stock you can burn through.

That third bucket is where most of the savings hide. Nobody needs to throw away nine hundred business cards on launch day. Physical items can transition gradually as long as the digital surfaces all flip at once.

## What to tell customers

The announcement gets overthought. It does not need to be a manifesto.

Tell them what changed, why, and what it means for them — which is usually "nothing about your service or your point of contact." That last part is the one customers actually care about. A rebrand can read as instability if you do not preempt it, especially in a relationship-driven market like this one.

Send it to existing customers a day before the public launch. Nobody who pays you should learn about your rebrand from Instagram. Brief your sales team a week early with the reasoning and a short answer to "so did you get acquired?" — because someone will ask.

## Takeaways

- Start in December so you launch ahead of Q1 convention season instead of during it
- Two or more honest yeses in Part 1 justify a rebrand; one means a refresh
- Positioning gets locked before anything visual gets designed
- Inventory every brand touchpoint early — that list is always longer than you think
- Launch all digital surfaces on one day and assign someone to own consistency after

## Bottom line

A rebrand is not a logo project. It is a decision about who you are for, expressed visually, applied everywhere, and defended for the next few years.

December gives you the runway to do it properly instead of rushing something forgettable in March. Use the window.
